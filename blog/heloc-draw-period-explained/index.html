HELOC Draw Period Explained: What Happens at End of Draw

The draw period is the part of a home equity line of credit (HELOC) during which you can generally borrow against the available credit line. When that period ends, new borrowing stops under the existing draw arrangement and the account moves into whatever repayment or maturity structure is specified in the agreement.

The important word is agreement. A ten-year draw period is common enough to appear in examples, but it is not a universal rule. Nor is every HELOC interest-only during draw or guaranteed to amortize over a particular number of years afterward. The lender's disclosures and contract determine the actual dates, payment formula and end-of-draw consequences.

What the HELOC draw period is

A HELOC is open-end credit secured by your home. CFPB explains that you can generally borrow up to the available credit limit during the borrowing period, also called the draw period. It gives ten years as an example of how long a draw period could last rather than presenting ten years as a rule.

Because a HELOC is revolving credit, repaying principal during draw can generally restore available credit, subject to the plan's terms and any lawful restrictions on further advances. Some plans also impose minimum initial draws, transaction minimums or minimum outstanding balances.

The contract matters more than the “typical HELOC”

Check the exact draw-period end date, borrowing rules, payment calculation, rate formula, fees and post-draw terms in your own HELOC agreement. Those details can differ materially between products.

Draw period vs repayment period

Phase 1

Draw Period

  • New borrowing generally permitted up to available credit
  • Principal can generally be repaid during draw
  • Minimum-payment formula depends on the plan
  • HELOC rates are usually variable
  • Some plans offer fixed-rate conversion features

Phase 2

After Draw

  • New draws stop under the existing draw period
  • Outstanding principal must be repaid according to the contract
  • Monthly payments are often significantly higher
  • Repayment may amortize over time
  • Some plans can require a balloon payment

CFPB says lenders may set a schedule that repays the full balance, often over ten or twenty years, but in some cases the whole amount borrowed can become due when repayment begins. The Federal Reserve's interagency end-of-draw guidance likewise describes HELOC portfolios with different post-draw characteristics, including amortizing payments and balloon payments.

How payments can work during the draw period

The original version of this guide treated every draw-period minimum as interest-only. That is too broad. CFPB says many HELOCs have minimum monthly payments based on the current balance, while Federal Reserve guidance says HELOCs often require interest-only payments during draw. Your agreement determines what you must pay.

If a particular plan does require interest-only payments, the interest portion for a simple monthly illustration can be estimated from the outstanding balance and annual rate. But real statements can be affected by daily-balance calculations, rate changes, billing-cycle length and the lender's payment formula, so a simplified calculation should not be treated as the contractual payment.

Principal payments can change the transition

If your plan allows additional principal payments during draw, reducing the outstanding balance can reduce the amount that remains when draw ends. Whether and how much extra to repay is a household financial decision; check for any plan-specific payment or prepayment terms rather than assuming a universal rule.

What happens when the draw period ends?

At end of draw, the central change is that further borrowing stops under the existing draw period. The outstanding balance then follows the repayment or maturity terms in the contract.

Payments can rise because principal that was not previously being amortized may now have to be repaid, because the repayment schedule can be shorter than the preceding draw period, because the interest rate may have changed, or because a balloon amount is due. CFPB says monthly payments are often significantly higher during repayment. Federal regulators use the term payment shock when discussing contractual increases that some borrowers can face at end of draw.

There is no universal “25%–80% payment shock”

The size of any increase depends on the outstanding balance, interest rate and contractual repayment terms. A fixed percentage range can create false precision. Use your lender's actual end-of-draw terms and a current balance to estimate the transition.

What the Federal Reserve guidance says

Federal Reserve interagency guidance tells supervised institutions to understand their HELOC end-of-draw exposures, communicate clearly with borrowers and prepare for borrowers who may face higher payments. It notes that outreach programs often begin at least six to nine months before scheduled end-of-draw dates. That is guidance to financial institutions—not a guarantee that every borrower will receive a particular notice exactly six or nine months in advance.

How to prepare for an approaching end-of-draw date

If your draw period is approaching its end, these are useful facts to establish before making decisions:

  • 1

    Confirm the contractual end date

    Check your agreement, statements or lender/servicer records for the exact date on which the draw period ends.

  • 2

    Read the post-draw payment terms

    Determine whether the remaining balance amortizes, over what period, whether a balloon payment can apply, and how the interest rate is determined.

  • 3

    Check your current balance and rate

    Those figures, together with the contract, are more useful for estimating future payments than a generic national example.

  • 4

    Ask the lender what alternatives actually exist

    Renewal, extension, refinancing, fixed-rate options or modifications are not guaranteed. Ask what your provider offers, what underwriting is required and what fees or new terms would apply.

  • 5

    Compare the projected payment with your budget

    If the contractual payment would be difficult to manage, contacting the lender or servicer before missed payments occur can provide more time to understand available options.

  • 6

    Review any further borrowing carefully

    Additional draws increase the balance that ultimately has to be repaid. Consider the effect of another draw on the post-draw balance rather than assuming available credit should be used.

Renewal, refinance and modification possibilities

There is no four-option menu that applies to every borrower. What is available depends on the existing contract, lender programs, underwriting, property value, borrower circumstances and market conditions.

Contractual repayment

The existing HELOC follows the repayment or maturity terms already agreed to. Review the payment calculation and any balloon provision.

Renewal or new HELOC

A lender may offer renewal, extension or a new line, but additional underwriting and new terms can apply. Approval is not automatic.

Refinancing

Depending on eligibility and available products, refinancing into another HELOC, a home equity loan or another loan may be possible. Compare the new rate, fees, collateral and term rather than assuming refinancing improves the outcome.

Workout or modification

For borrowers experiencing difficulty, some institutions may have modification or workout programs. Federal guidance encourages sustainable programs where feasible, but the options available to an individual borrower vary.

The Federal Reserve's end-of-draw guidance specifically discusses renewals, extensions, rewrites, workouts and modifications as possibilities institutions may manage. It does not promise that any particular option will be offered to every borrower.

Variable rates and fixed-rate conversion

HELOCs usually have variable rates. A common structure uses an index plus a margin, but the contract determines the index and margin. Prime is commonly used as an index, but it is not universal, and a Federal Reserve benchmark should not be confused with a nationwide average HELOC offer.

Some HELOCs let borrowers convert some or all of an outstanding variable-rate balance to a fixed rate. CFPB notes that the fixed rate is usually higher than the variable rate but is more predictable. Conversion availability, timing, minimum amounts and fees are product-specific.
