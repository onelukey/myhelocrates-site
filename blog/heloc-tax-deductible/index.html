Is HELOC Interest Tax-Deductible? What IRS Guidance Says

HELOC interest may be deductible for U.S. federal income-tax purposes in some circumstances. The IRS focuses on factors including what the borrowed money was used for, whether the debt is secured by a qualified home, applicable debt limits and whether the taxpayer itemizes deductions.

This page is not a tax opinion and it does not try to decide whether your interest is deductible. Its job is to translate the current IRS material into plain English and point you to the official sources you can use to verify it.

The short answer

The IRS currently says that, for tax years beginning after 2017, interest on a home equity loan or HELOC secured by a main home or second home may be deductible when the borrowed funds are used to buy, build or substantially improve the residence securing the loan, subject to applicable dollar limits. Interest on the same debt used for personal living expenses, such as credit-card debt, is not deductible as home-mortgage interest.

Start with the IRS's own answer

The IRS FAQ answers “Is interest paid on a home equity loan or HELOC deductible?” with: “It depends.” That is a better starting point than a blanket yes or no.

Mortgage-interest deductions are itemized deductions reported on Schedule A. Whether itemizing produces a tax benefit depends on the taxpayer's wider return, filing status and other deductions. A HELOC being potentially eligible for mortgage-interest treatment does not by itself tell you the tax result.

An important 2026 source note

As of this article's September 26, 2026 review, the IRS lists Publication 936 (2025), Home Mortgage Interest Deduction as the current revision, and the publication states that it is for preparing 2025 returns. The IRS also directs readers to its Publication 936 page for future developments and legislation enacted after publication.

That distinction matters. Rather than presenting a 2025 publication as though it were a complete 2026 tax manual, this guide identifies the tax year attached to each source. The IRS has separately published tax-year 2026 inflation adjustments, including the 2026 standard deduction amounts.

Why we do not call these rules “permanent”

Tax law can be amended by future legislation. Current law may remove an earlier scheduled expiration without making a rule literally unchangeable. We therefore describe what current IRS guidance says and date the source instead of promising that a tax rule is permanent.

Why the use of the HELOC funds matters

Current IRS guidance distinguishes between home-secured borrowing used for qualifying home-acquisition purposes and borrowing used for personal expenses. The IRS FAQ specifically gives credit-card debt as an example of a personal use for which the interest is not deductible as home-mortgage interest.

Potentially qualifying use

Funds used to buy, build or substantially improve the main or second home that secures the debt, subject to the other requirements and limitations in IRS guidance.

Personal-use example

The IRS specifically says interest on home-equity borrowing used for personal living expenses such as credit-card debt is not deductible as home-mortgage interest.

This is why the label on the product—HELOC, home equity loan or another home-secured loan—is not enough to determine deductibility. How proceeds were used matters.

Home-acquisition debt limits

Publication 936 (2025) says home-mortgage interest can generally be deducted on the first $750,000 of qualifying indebtedness, or $375,000 for married taxpayers filing separately. It also describes higher limits for certain qualifying debt incurred before December 16, 2017.

Those figures are not a simple “HELOC allowance.” The publication contains rules for determining qualified loan limits when a taxpayer has debt from different dates or multiple mortgages. If a limit may affect you, use the Publication 936 worksheet and current tax-year instructions or seek qualified tax help rather than simply adding balances to a generic $750,000 ceiling.

Do not treat $750,000 as a universal three-condition test

Debt dates, filing status, how proceeds were used and other mortgage-interest rules can affect the calculation. Publication 936 contains the detailed worksheet.

Itemizing and the standard deduction

Home-mortgage interest is generally claimed as an itemized deduction on Schedule A. A taxpayer who takes the standard deduction does not separately add a mortgage-interest itemized deduction on top of it.

For tax year 2026, the IRS has published these basic standard-deduction amounts:

Filing status2026 standard deduction
Single$16,100
Married filing separately$16,100
Married filing jointly / qualifying surviving spouse$32,200
Head of household$24,150

Source: IRS tax-year 2026 inflation adjustments. Additional rules and additional deduction amounts can apply in some circumstances, so these figures should not be used as a complete return calculation.

The practical comparison is between the standard deduction available on a particular return and the taxpayer's allowable itemized deductions as a whole. We have deliberately removed the old fictional tax-return example because a simplified scenario can look like personalized tax guidance while omitting rules that may change the result.

What does “substantially improve” mean?

Publication 936 (2025) says an improvement is substantial if it adds to the value of the home, prolongs its useful life or adapts it to new uses. It also says repairs that maintain the home in good condition, such as repainting, are not substantial improvements.

That definition is more reliable than publishing our own checklist saying that every roof, HVAC system, bathroom, solar installation or structural repair automatically qualifies. The facts and how the borrowed proceeds were used matter.

Examples are not tax rulings

A project can contain repair, maintenance and improvement elements. If classification matters to your return, keep the project records and use current IRS instructions or a qualified tax professional rather than relying on a website's yes/no list.

Records and HELOCs used for more than one purpose

Publication 936 and Schedule A instructions make the use of loan proceeds important to the deduction calculation. If HELOC proceeds are used for different purposes, the deductible amount can require more detailed tracing and calculation than simply applying one percentage to the year's total interest.

Useful records can include lender statements and tax forms, draw records, invoices, receipts and payment records showing where borrowed funds went. The records appropriate to a particular return depend on the facts and IRS recordkeeping requirements.

Form 1098 can report mortgage interest received by a lender, but receiving a Form 1098 does not by itself establish that every dollar reported is deductible. The taxpayer still has to apply the relevant mortgage-interest rules.

Mixed-use HELOC?

Do not rely on the old shortcut that said “two-thirds of the borrowing was for improvements, so two-thirds of the interest is deductible.” Timing, balances and tracing can matter. Publication 936 and the Schedule A instructions are the better starting points, and a tax professional can help with a mixed-use calculation.
