How to Find a Lower HELOC Rate

Last reviewed: September 25, 2026

There is no single lender or advertised percentage that is permanently the lowest HELOC rate. HELOC pricing changes with market conditions, lender products and the borrower's application. A useful comparison therefore starts with understanding how each offer is priced rather than chasing a number that may be temporary or unavailable to you.

The practical goal

Compare the same pieces across real offers: the index, margin, introductory period, fully indexed rate, rate limits, fees and payment rules. An advertised starting APR by itself does not tell you the long-term cost of a HELOC.

What “lowest HELOC rate” really means

HELOCs usually have variable interest rates. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that a variable rate generally consists of an index plus a margin. The index can move over time; the margin is specified by the lender's plan.

That means two offers displaying similar rates today can behave differently later. One may have an introductory rate that expires, a different margin, a different index, or different rate caps and fees.

The Federal Reserve's H.15 release publishes the Bank Prime Loan rate, which is commonly used as a HELOC index. Prime is a benchmark, however — not a nationwide average HELOC offer and not the index used by every plan.

For current benchmark context, see our HELOC Rates & Prime Rate page.

How HELOC pricing works

CFPB's HELOC brochure explains that lenders may use indexes such as the U.S. prime rate or a Constant Maturity Treasury rate. The lender then adds or subtracts the margin specified in the plan to determine the variable rate, subject to the plan's other terms.

Illustration — not a market quote

If a hypothetical HELOC used an index of 6.75% and a margin of +0.75 percentage points, the resulting rate would be 7.50% before considering any introductory pricing or other contractual provisions. The 0.75-point margin here is purely an example; it is not presented as a typical lender margin.

When comparing variable-rate HELOCs, the margin and index matter because today's headline rate can change. CFPB also advises consumers to ask how much the rate can change, how often it can change and what the maximum rate could be.

Factors that can affect the rate you are offered

Lenders set their own underwriting and pricing criteria. Depending on the lender and product, the offer can reflect credit history or credit score, income and debts, property value, existing liens, requested credit line, occupancy or property characteristics, and other permitted factors.

There is no reliable industry-wide table saying that a particular credit score automatically produces a particular HELOC rate. That is why we removed the old 620/700/760 rate bands from this article.

Similarly, there is no universal rule that a particular CLTV or DTI automatically earns a specific discount. A lender may use these measures in underwriting or pricing, but its thresholds and rate adjustments are product-specific.

If you want to understand the underwriting categories in more detail, see How to Qualify for a HELOC and What Credit Score Do You Need for a HELOC?.

How to compare lenders

Getting more than one offer can show you how lenders price the same application differently. But comparison shopping is most useful when you compare like with like rather than simply choosing the lowest number in an advertisement.

Ask each lender for the current disclosures for the product you are considering. A lender's published “as low as” APR can depend on assumptions or eligibility conditions that may not match your application.

What about credit inquiries while shopping?

Do not rely on a blanket “14-day rule.” Credit-scoring treatment depends on the scoring model and the type of inquiry. CFPB recommends asking lenders whether they will make a hard inquiry and notes that multiple inquiries for some types of loans within a certain period can be treated as a single inquiry. If minimizing inquiries matters to you, ask each lender what kind of credit check it will use before authorizing it.

Introductory rates and relationship discounts

Some lenders advertise introductory HELOC rates or discounts linked to conditions such as automatic payments or an existing banking relationship. These can affect an offer, but the details vary by lender.

For an introductory rate, check the expiration date and the formula used afterward. For a relationship or autopay discount, verify the eligibility requirements, whether the discount can be lost and whether maintaining another account introduces costs of its own.

We removed the old claim that banks “regularly” give a specific 0.25–0.50 percentage-point relationship discount because there is no universal discount. If a lender offers one, its current disclosure is the source of truth.

Rates are only part of the cost

CFPB says HELOCs can carry application fees, origination or closing costs, appraisal or title costs, annual or membership fees, inactivity fees, early-cancellation fees and fixed-rate conversion fees. Some plans may also require an initial advance, minimum transaction amount or minimum outstanding balance.

A “no closing cost” HELOC is therefore not automatically cheaper, and a product with a slightly lower advertised rate is not automatically the lower-cost choice. Compare the costs that actually apply to your expected use of the line.

We have deliberately removed generic dollar ranges for annual fees, closing costs, early-closure charges and minimum draws from this article. Those figures belong in the lender's current disclosures, not in a permanent site-wide rule.

Fixed-rate conversion features

HELOCs usually have variable rates, but CFPB notes that some plans allow a borrower to convert some or all of the outstanding balance to a fixed rate. Availability, conversion fees, minimum amounts, fixed-rate terms and the number of conversions permitted can differ by lender.

A fixed-rate feature changes rate exposure; it does not guarantee that the resulting rate will be lower. Compare the conversion terms with the variable-rate terms and any alternative product you are considering.

Sources & methodology

This guide focuses on durable comparison principles rather than maintaining a “lowest rate” leaderboard. We use primary government sources for HELOC mechanics and consumer guidance, and we treat lender-specific pricing as something that must be checked against current lender disclosures.
