How Much Home Equity Do You Need for a HELOC?

Home equity matters when you apply for a HELOC, but there is no single percentage of equity that every lender requires. The lender decides how much credit it is willing to extend using its product rules and underwriting, including the value it accepts for the property and the debt already secured by the home.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes available home equity as the home's value minus the amount owed on the mortgage. Its HELOC guidance also describes a HELOC credit limit as generally being based on a percentage of the home's appraised value, less the amount owed on the first mortgage. The percentage itself is lender-specific.

Why you will not see a universal “20% equity required” rule here

Our older version treated 80%, 85% and 90% CLTV figures as standard lender categories. That was too broad. Lenders and products differ, so this guide teaches the calculation and lets you insert the maximum CLTV from an actual lender's current terms.

What is home equity?

In simple terms, home equity is the difference between the current value of your home and debt secured by it.

Simple equity example

If a home is worth $450,000 and the outstanding mortgage balance is $280,000, the homeowner has $170,000 of equity before considering any other debt secured by the property. That is about 37.8% of the home's value.

Equity can change as mortgage principal is repaid and as the property's market value rises or falls. Having equity, however, does not mean the entire amount is available to borrow. The lender sets the maximum line it is prepared to offer and evaluates the application under its own criteria.

Is there a minimum equity requirement for a HELOC?

There is no universal HELOC rule requiring every homeowner to retain exactly 10%, 15% or 20% equity. A lender can set its own maximum loan-to-value or combined loan-to-value limits for a particular product, subject to applicable law and its underwriting policies.

That is why two lenders can potentially calculate different maximum credit lines for the same property and mortgage balance. Product availability can also depend on factors such as property type, location, requested line amount and the rest of the borrower's application.

If a lender publishes a maximum CLTV, use that lender's current figure in the calculation below. Do not assume a percentage found in a general article applies to your application.

Understanding CLTV

Combined loan-to-value (CLTV) compares debt secured by the property with the property's value. CFPB's HMDA commentary describes CLTV as the ratio of the total amount of debt secured by the property to the value of the property when a financial institution relies on that ratio in making its credit decision.

Illustrative CLTV formula

CLTV = total debt secured by the home ÷ home value × 100

For a simple first-mortgage-plus-HELOC example: ($200,000 mortgage + $80,000 HELOC limit) ÷ $400,000 home value × 100 = 70% CLTV.

The 70% result is only the calculation. Whether it is acceptable depends on the lender and product.

If there are other liens or debts secured by the property, a lender's calculation can be more involved. Use the lender's definition and underwriting method when evaluating an actual application.

Illustrative HELOC limit

The basic educational calculation is:

  1. Home value × assumed lender maximum CLTV = illustrative total secured-debt ceiling.
  2. Illustrative ceiling − existing property-secured debt = illustrative remaining amount.

A lender can approve less, decline the application, use a different property value or apply other underwriting requirements. The result is not an offer or qualification estimate.

A worked example

Suppose a home is valued at $400,000, existing debt secured by it is $200,000, and you are examining a lender product that states an 80% maximum CLTV for the circumstances you are researching.

StepIllustrative calculation
Home value$400,000
Assumed lender maximum CLTV80% — example only
Illustrative secured-debt ceiling$400,000 × 80% = $320,000
Existing secured debt$200,000
Illustrative remaining amount$320,000 − $200,000 = $120,000

This example demonstrates the arithmetic; it does not mean 80% is a standard HELOC maximum or that a lender would approve a $120,000 line.

Why the lender's property value matters

The estimated line can change materially if the value used by the lender changes. If a property is valued higher, the same existing secured debt represents a lower percentage of that value. If the property is valued lower, the opposite is true.

That does not mean homeowners should assume appreciation or make improvements specifically to manufacture HELOC eligibility. Market values can move in either direction, and the lender determines the value it will rely on for the application.

Online estimates are planning tools, not lender decisions

A consumer-facing home-value estimate can be useful for rough planning, but it does not establish the value a particular HELOC lender will use. Ask the lender how it will determine property value and whether any valuation fee applies.

How property value may be established

Valuation procedures vary. Depending on the lender, transaction and applicable requirements, the process may involve an appraisal, another form of property evaluation or an automated valuation model (AVM). Do not assume every HELOC requires a full interior appraisal or that a particular valuation method has a standard nationwide price.

CFPB specifically lists appraisal costs among the closing costs a HELOC lender might charge, and HELOC application disclosures include estimates of certain third-party closing fees such as appraisal fees. Check the lender's disclosures for the method and costs relevant to your application.

Equity is only one part of underwriting

Enough equity does not guarantee HELOC approval. A lender may consider credit information, income, existing debts and obligations, the requested line, property information and other permitted underwriting factors.

Likewise, there is no site-wide rule that every HELOC requires a 620 credit score, a DTI below 43%, two years of a particular income document or a specific recent mortgage-payment history. Those requirements depend on the lender and product.
