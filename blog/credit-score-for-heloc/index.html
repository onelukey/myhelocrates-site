What Credit Score Do You Need for a HELOC?

There is no single credit score that guarantees HELOC approval across US lenders. A lender may use credit scores and credit-report information when deciding whether to offer credit and on what terms, but its underwriting standards are lender- and product-specific.

That makes a question such as “Do I need a 620 score?” less useful than it first appears. A published minimum from one lender is not a universal HELOC rule, and meeting a stated score threshold does not by itself guarantee approval or a particular interest rate.

No universal HELOC credit-score cutoff

The CFPB explains that credit scores can be used in lending decisions and pricing, but consumers have more than one credit score and scores can differ by scoring model, data source and date. Check the requirements of the specific lender and HELOC product you are considering.

Is there a minimum credit score for a HELOC?

There is no CFPB-established minimum credit score that applies to every HELOC lender. Individual lenders can set their own underwriting criteria, subject to applicable lending laws.

You may see particular lenders publish minimum scores or preferred credit profiles. Treat those as product-specific requirements rather than a market-wide rule. Lender policies can also change, so verify current requirements directly with the lender before relying on a number quoted elsewhere.

Our broader HELOC qualification guide explains why credit is only one part of the application.

How lenders may use credit scores

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes a credit score as a prediction of credit behaviour based on information in your credit reports. Companies can use scores when deciding whether to offer credit and when determining an interest rate or credit limit.

A higher score may make it easier to qualify for credit and may result in better terms, but it does not create a guaranteed HELOC rate. The lender can evaluate other information in the application, and different lenders can reach different decisions.

Credit informationWhat it can tell a lender
Payment historyHow accounts have been paid over time.
Current unpaid debtHow much debt is already outstanding.
Credit utilisationHow much available revolving credit is being used.
Account historyThe age and mix of accounts in the credit file.
Recent applicationsHow recently and frequently new credit has been sought.
Serious negative eventsCollections, foreclosure or bankruptcy information may affect a score depending on the model and timing.

These are examples of factors the CFPB says credit-scoring models typically consider. They are not a formula for predicting whether a HELOC application will be approved.

What lenders review beyond your score

A HELOC is secured by your home, so underwriting is broader than a credit score alone. Depending on the lender and product, the application may involve your income, debts, property value, existing liens, requested credit line and other information used to assess the application.

FactorWhy it may matter
Home equity and existing liensHelp determine how much equity is available to secure additional borrowing.
IncomeCan help the lender evaluate your ability to repay.
Existing debtsProvide context for your current financial obligations.
Property informationThe lender may need to establish the property's value and eligibility.

Credit historyThe lender may review reports, scores and payment history as part of its underwriting process.

There is no universal CLTV, DTI, employment-history or credit-score combination that applies to every lender. Verify the criteria for the particular HELOC you are considering.

What to check before applying

You do not need to chase an arbitrary number of credit-score points before you can learn more about your position. A more useful preparation process is to make sure the information lenders may review is accurate and to understand your existing obligations.

  • 1

    Review your credit reports

    Check your reports for information you do not recognise or believe is inaccurate. Requesting your own credit report is a soft inquiry and does not affect your credit score.

  • 2

    Dispute genuine errors

    If you find inaccurate information, use the credit reporting company's dispute process. Do not dispute accurate negative information simply in an attempt to remove it.

  • 3

    Keep payments current

    Payment history is one of the factors typically considered by credit-scoring models. If possible, avoid creating new late payments while preparing an application.

  • 4

    Understand your outstanding debts

    List your existing obligations and balances so you can evaluate the proposed HELOC alongside the debts you already have.

  • 5

    Ask lenders how their process works

    Before submitting a full application, ask what information the lender can provide without a hard credit inquiry and when it expects to obtain your credit report.

You can obtain your credit reports through AnnualCreditReport.com. The CFPB also explains that consumers can currently request reports from the nationwide credit reporting companies weekly.

Credit-score changes are not predictable point-for-point

A specific action does not guarantee a particular score increase or a fixed improvement timeline. The result depends on the scoring model and the information already in your credit file. That is why this guide does not promise “20 points,” “50 points,” or a particular score by a particular date.

HELOC applications and credit inquiries

CFPB says lenders usually obtain a credit report when you apply for new credit. This is generally a hard inquiry and can affect a credit score. Asking a lender about rates, by itself, does not give the lender permission to pull your credit report.

Do not assume that every HELOC inquiry will automatically be grouped into a single inquiry under a fixed “14-day rule.” Credit-scoring treatment depends on the scoring model and how the inquiry is coded. If you plan to compare several lenders, ask each lender when a hard inquiry will occur.

If a HELOC application is denied

If a lender denies an application for a loan or line of credit, CFPB says the lender must provide the main reasons for the decision or tell you how to obtain them. If the decision was based on your credit report, additional information about the credit reporting company and the score used must also be provided.

That information is more useful than guessing that a particular score was the problem. It can help you identify whether the decision related to credit history, debts, income, property information or another factor in the lender's underwriting.
