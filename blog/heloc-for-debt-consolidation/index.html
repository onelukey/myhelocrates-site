Using a HELOC for Debt Consolidation: Risks & Trade-Offs

Using a HELOC to consolidate debt means borrowing against your home and using the proceeds to pay other debts, such as credit-card balances or personal loans. A HELOC may carry a different interest rate and payment structure, but comparing rates alone misses the most important change: debt that was previously unsecured can become debt secured by your home.

That does not make debt consolidation with a HELOC automatically appropriate or inappropriate. It means the comparison needs to account for collateral risk, variable rates, fees, repayment structure and the possibility of building new balances after the original debts are paid off.

How HELOC debt consolidation works

A HELOC is an open-end line of credit secured by your home. During the draw period, you can generally borrow up to the available limit under the terms of the plan. A draw can then be used to pay other debts, with the HELOC repaid according to its agreement.

The exact payment structure is plan-specific. It is not safe to assume that every HELOC has a ten-year draw period or that every draw-period payment is interest-only. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that many HELOCs have minimum monthly payments based on the current balance and that borrowers can make payments during the draw period. After draw ends, new borrowing stops and payments are often significantly higher during repayment; some plans can require the outstanding balance to be repaid when repayment begins.

Where to verify the mechanics

The CFPB HELOC explainer covers draw periods, repayment periods, variable rates, fees and possible restrictions on additional borrowing. Your lender's agreement controls the terms of your HELOC.

The central trade-off: cost versus collateral

The attraction of consolidation is straightforward: if the HELOC offer available to you has a lower borrowing cost than the debt being repaid, moving the balance can reduce interest expense. But the useful comparison is based on your actual debts and your actual HELOC offer—not a temporary national-average rate gap.

The risk changes at the same time. Credit-card debt is generally unsecured. A HELOC is secured by the home. CFPB warns that if you fall behind on HELOC payments, you could lose the home. That collateral difference belongs beside any potential interest saving in the comparison.

Moving debt is not the same as eliminating debt

Paying a credit-card balance with HELOC proceeds moves the balance to another form of borrowing. If new card balances are then accumulated, the household can end up carrying both the HELOC balance and new unsecured debt.

Risks to understand

1. Your home secures the HELOC

This is the defining risk difference. A HELOC gives the lender a security interest in the home. Failure to meet the agreement can therefore have consequences that unsecured credit-card borrowing does not have.

2. The HELOC rate may change

HELOCs usually have variable rates, so the interest rate and payment can change. Some plans allow some or all of a balance to be converted to a fixed rate, but availability and terms vary. Do not assume the rate when the line opens will remain unchanged.

3. Draw-period payments can hide the eventual repayment burden

CFPB notes that monthly payments are often significantly higher after the draw period ends. Compare what you may owe during repayment, not just the initial minimum payment.

4. Fees can reduce an apparent saving

Depending on the plan, HELOC costs can include application, origination, appraisal or other closing costs, annual or membership fees, inactivity fees, early-cancellation fees and fixed-rate conversion fees. Review the lender's current fee schedule.

5. Additional borrowing can be restricted

In circumstances permitted by applicable law and the agreement, a lender may freeze or reduce access to additional HELOC credit. Our HELOC freeze guide explains this using Regulation Z and CFPB sources.

6. Cleared revolving accounts can be borrowed on again

If consolidation frees up credit-card limits, those accounts may remain available for new spending unless they are closed or otherwise restricted. Whether to close an account involves separate considerations; this article does not prescribe a one-size-fits-all approach.

Alternatives to compare

A HELOC is only one way to restructure debt. Depending on the offers and assistance available, other possibilities can include a home equity loan, personal loan, balance-transfer card or a credit-counseling/debt-management arrangement. These structures are not interchangeable.

Questions to ask before proceeding

  • What balance am I moving? Use current statements rather than an estimate.
  • What will this HELOC actually cost? Record its index, margin, introductory terms, fees and payment rules.
  • How quickly would I repay it? Compare a realistic payoff plan rather than only the required minimum.
  • Could I handle a higher variable rate or repayment-period payment? Test the household budget rather than assuming today's payment persists.
  • What happens to the accounts I pay off? Consider the risk of rebuilding balances after consolidation.
  • Am I comfortable securing this debt with my home? That is the central risk change regardless of the rate comparison.

Tax treatment of a debt-consolidation HELOC

Current IRS guidance says interest on a home equity loan or HELOC used to pay personal living expenses such as credit-card debt is not deductible as home mortgage interest. Interest may be deductible when qualifying borrowed funds are used to buy, build or substantially improve the home securing the debt, subject to the other requirements and limits.

The latest IRS Publication 936 available when this article was reviewed is the 2025 edition for preparing 2025 returns. Because tax rules can change, this page does not assume that a historical rule automatically remains unchanged for a later tax year. Check current IRS guidance or a qualified tax professional for the return you are preparing.
