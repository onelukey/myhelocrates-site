HELOC vs Home Equity Loan — How They Differ

A HELOC and a home equity loan both let a homeowner borrow against home equity, and both use the home as collateral. The main difference is the borrowing structure: a HELOC is a reusable line of credit during its draw period, while a home equity loan provides a specific amount as a lump sum.

That structural difference affects how you receive the money, how interest is charged, how predictable payments may be and how much flexibility you have after closing. Neither product is automatically the better choice for every borrower.

Start with the structure, not a “winner”

If you are comparing these products, ask whether you need repeated access to funds or one defined amount, then compare the actual rate structure, fees, payment rules and risks in the lender disclosures.

The key structural difference

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes a home equity loan as a specific amount borrowed against your home's equity, generally received as a lump sum. A HELOC is an open-end line of credit that allows repeated draws from an available maximum amount.

If you already have a first mortgage, CFPB notes that either product would generally be an additional mortgage secured by the property. Because your home is collateral, failure to repay either type of borrowing can put the home at risk.

How each product works

HELOC

During a HELOC's draw period, you can generally borrow up to the available credit limit, repay amounts and borrow again subject to the plan's terms. Interest is generally based on the outstanding balance rather than unused credit.

When the draw period ends, new borrowing stops and the repayment period begins. CFPB says the lender may set a schedule to repay the balance, often over a number of years, while some plans can require the full amount when repayment begins. The actual agreement controls.

Home equity loan

A home equity loan provides the borrowed amount as a lump sum. Repayment begins according to the loan's schedule, and interest applies to the outstanding loan balance.

CFPB says home equity loans usually have fixed interest rates. That distinction matters: “usually fixed” is more accurate than assuming every home equity loan must have a fixed rate, because CFPB also notes that a home equity loan may have a fixed or adjustable rate.

Interest-rate structure

HELOCs usually have variable rates. CFPB explains that a variable HELOC rate generally combines an index with a margin. Different lenders can use different indexes; common examples include the U.S. prime rate and the Constant Maturity Treasury rate.

The Federal Reserve's H.15 release publishes the Bank Prime Loan rate, but Prime should not be treated as a universal HELOC index or as an average HELOC offer. The product disclosures tell you which index and margin apply.

Home equity loans usually have fixed rates, according to CFPB. A fixed rate can make scheduled payments more predictable, while a variable-rate HELOC can change as its index changes. Some HELOCs also offer a feature that lets a borrower convert some or all of a balance to a fixed rate; the terms and possible fees are plan-specific.

How payments differ

A HELOC payment can change because the outstanding balance changes as you borrow or repay, because a variable rate changes, and because the payment formula can change when the draw period ends. CFPB warns that monthly payments are often significantly higher in the repayment period.

Home equity loan payments depend on the amount borrowed, interest rate, term and loan structure. A fixed-rate, fully amortizing home equity loan can provide a predictable scheduled payment, but borrowers should verify the actual disclosures rather than assume every home equity loan has identical terms.

For a meaningful comparison, use the same amount and time horizon and examine the lender's actual APR, rate structure, fees and payment schedule. A made-up comparison using two temporary “national average” rates can create false precision.

Access to funds and flexibility

The HELOC's revolving structure can be useful when the amount or timing of future borrowing is uncertain. If you repay part of the balance during the draw period, available credit is generally replenished, subject to the plan and any lawful restrictions on further advances.

A home equity loan works differently: the borrower receives the loan proceeds as a lump sum rather than maintaining a revolving line for future draws.

That does not mean an unused HELOC is a guaranteed emergency fund. CFPB notes that lenders can restrict further advances in certain circumstances, including a significant decline in the home's value or qualifying changes in the borrower's financial circumstances. Questions to compare before choosing either structure

QuestionWhy it matters
Do I need one amount or repeated access?This is the basic structural difference between a lump-sum loan and a revolving line.
Is the rate fixed, adjustable or convertible?Rate structure affects payment predictability and exposure to future rate changes.
How is the minimum payment calculated?HELOC payment rules can differ between the draw and repayment periods.
What are the total fees?Closing, annual, transaction, early-termination and conversion charges can change the comparison.
What happens at the end of the draw period?Repayment terms can materially change the required payment.
How much secured debt can I comfortably repay?Both products put the home at risk if the debt cannot be repaid.

These questions are more useful than a blanket rule saying that a HELOC “wins” for renovations or a home equity loan “wins” for a known expense. The actual terms and the borrower's circumstances determine the trade-offs.

Fees and other costs

Costs vary by lender and product. CFPB says HELOC plans can include application fees, origination or closing costs, appraisal or title costs, annual or membership fees, inactivity fees, early-cancellation fees and fixed-rate conversion fees.

CFPB also notes that home equity loans may have upfront fees and costs. When comparing products, look beyond the monthly payment and compare the disclosures for the specific offers you are considering.

Compare actual disclosures, not assumed fee ranges

We removed the old dollar ranges from this article because fees are product-specific. A lender advertising “no closing costs” may also have conditions or other charges that matter to the total cost.

Tax considerations

Federal tax treatment can depend on how borrowed funds are used and on other requirements. Current IRS guidance says interest on a home equity loan or HELOC secured by a main or second home may be deductible when the borrowed funds are used to buy, build or substantially improve the residence, subject to applicable requirements and limits. Interest on the same debt used for personal living expenses, such as paying credit-card debt, is not deductible under that guidance.

The latest IRS Publication 936 available when this page was reviewed is the edition for preparing 2025 returns. Because tax law and tax-year guidance can change, we do not treat that publication as a blanket statement about every future tax year. Check current IRS material or a qualified tax professional for your own filing year and circumstances.
