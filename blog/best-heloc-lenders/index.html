Best HELOC Lenders? How to Compare HELOC Providers

There is no single HELOC lender that is best for every homeowner. A lender with an attractive advertised rate may have different fees, eligibility rules, draw requirements, geographic availability or repayment terms from another provider. The useful comparison is the one based on the offers and terms available to you.

This guide therefore does not rank lenders from best to worst. Instead, it shows what to compare and uses current lender information as examples of how HELOC products can differ. For general HELOC mechanics, we rely primarily on the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB); for lender-specific terms, the lender's own disclosures are the source of truth.

How this comparison works

Our comparison method

We do not assign scores, winners or “best for” labels. We compare the features that can materially change the cost or usefulness of a HELOC and link to primary lender information where a lender example is discussed.

Rate structureIndex, margin, introductory pricing, discounts and rate limits
FeesApplication, closing, annual, inactivity, cancellation and conversion charges
Equity requirementsThe lender's property and CLTV rules for the requested product
Access to fundsInitial-draw rules, transaction minimums and ways to access the line
Draw & repaymentPeriod lengths, payment formula and any fixed-rate conversion feature
EligibilityCredit underwriting, membership, property type and geographic availability

What to compare between HELOC lenders

CFPB recommends reading HELOC disclosures carefully because plans can differ in both cost and operation. Depending on the plan, charges can include application, origination, appraisal, title or other closing costs, annual or membership fees, inactivity fees, early-cancellation fees and fixed-rate conversion fees.

Examples of how lender terms can differ

Rather than maintain a permanent leaderboard, here are examples of differences visible in lender-published material. These examples are snapshots, not recommendations. Check the linked lender pages for current terms.

Bank of America

Example: introductory pricing, discounts and fixed-rate conversion

Bank of America's current HELOC material illustrates several features worth checking at any lender. Its rate page publishes a temporary introductory variable APR followed by an ongoing variable APR, and describes discounts tied to automatic payments, initial withdrawals and eligible rewards status. Its HELOC page currently states that there is no application fee, no annual fee and no closing costs on lines up to $1 million, while its disclosures also describe an early-closure charge in applicable circumstances.

Bank of America also advertises a fixed-rate loan option that can convert qualifying portions of an outstanding variable-rate HELOC balance to a fixed rate. That is a useful reminder to compare not just today's APR but the options available after the line is open.

Verify current Bank of America HELOC information.

Why we no longer publish a “winner” table

Rates and promotions change, underwriting is borrower-specific, and an advertised feature may apply only under particular conditions. A static ranking can become misleading quickly. Our job is to show you what to inspect; the lender's current disclosures tell you what is actually being offered.

Credit unions and membership-based providers

Credit unions can have membership requirements in addition to ordinary underwriting and property rules. If you are comparing a credit union, confirm that you are eligible to join and that its HELOC is offered for your property location. Our separate credit-union HELOC comparison uses the same no-ranking approach and links to current provider information.

Digital and non-bank providers

Products marketed as digital HELOCs can differ materially from traditional bank lines in funding mechanics, initial-draw requirements, fees and how money is accessed. Do not assume that two products carrying the “HELOC” label operate identically. Read the agreement and disclosures for the exact product you are considering.

How to compare HELOC rates

HELOCs usually have variable interest rates. CFPB explains that payments can change from month to month and that some HELOCs allow some or all of a balance to be converted from a variable rate to a fixed rate.

A headline APR by itself may not tell the whole story. Determine whether it is introductory or ongoing, which index applies, what margin is added, whether discounts depend on opening draws or linked accounts, and whether the advertised assumptions resemble your circumstances.

For example, Bank of America's rate page currently separates its introductory APR from its later variable APR and says its displayed rates assume excellent credit. That is much more informative than copying a single advertised number into a permanent lender ranking.

For current benchmark context, see our HELOC Rates & Prime Rate page. We keep temporary rate information there rather than duplicating national-average figures throughout the site.

How to compare HELOC fees

Do not assume “no closing costs” means “no possible fees.” CFPB lists several categories that can apply under HELOC plans, including annual, inactivity, cancellation and conversion fees as well as costs associated with opening the account.

Ask each provider for the fees that apply to the line you are actually considering. Also check whether the lender pays or waives costs only if the account remains open for a specified period. A waived upfront cost can therefore still matter later if you refinance or close the line early.

Draw, repayment and fixed-rate options

CFPB describes the draw period as the period in which you can generally borrow up to the available credit limit, and uses ten years as an example rather than a universal term. After draw ends, new borrowing stops and repayment begins; payments are often significantly higher, and some plans can require the outstanding balance to be repaid when repayment begins.

Compare the actual draw period, repayment period and payment calculation shown in each lender's disclosures. If fixed-rate conversion matters to you, check exactly how it works rather than assuming the entire HELOC can be permanently “locked.”

Eligibility and availability

HELOC approval and pricing are lender-specific. A provider may consider property value and existing liens, credit history, income, debts and other permitted underwriting factors. Some providers also have geographic, property-type or membership restrictions.

That is why this page does not use a universal 620 score, 80–85% CLTV band or similar “standard” threshold. A lender may publish a particular requirement for its own product—as Bank of America currently does on its HELOC page—but that does not turn the lender's rule into an industry-wide rule.

See What Credit Score Do You Need for a HELOC?, How Much Equity Do You Need? and How to Qualify for a HELOC for the underlying concepts.

CFPB's HELOC materials are designed to help consumers shop around and identify potential pitfalls. Use the lender's formal disclosures—not a comparison site's summary—as the final check before opening a line.

Remember what secures the line

A HELOC is secured by your home. CFPB warns that if you fall behind or cannot repay according to the agreement, you could lose the home. The size of a credit line a lender is willing to approve is not the same thing as an amount that is comfortable for your household to repay.
