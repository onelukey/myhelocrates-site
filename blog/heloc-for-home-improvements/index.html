Using a HELOC for Home Improvements: What to Know

A home equity line of credit (HELOC) can be used to pay for home improvements, but the fact that a project improves the property does not automatically make a HELOC the right financing choice. A HELOC is debt secured by the home, usually carries a variable interest rate, and has product-specific fees and repayment rules.

Its main structural feature for renovation spending is flexibility: during the draw period, you can generally borrow from the available line more than once rather than receiving the entire amount as a single lump sum. That can match projects in which deposits, materials and contractor payments occur at different stages.

Start with the project, then compare the financing

Build a realistic project budget and payment schedule first. Then compare the HELOC's rate structure, fees, draw rules and future repayment requirements with other financing available to you. The credit limit is a borrowing ceiling, not a renovation budget.

Why a HELOC can fit staged renovation spending

A HELOC is an open-end line of credit. CFPB says borrowers can generally draw repeatedly from the available line during the draw period, and repayments can replenish available credit. This differs from a home equity loan, which generally provides a specific amount as a lump sum.

For a renovation that is paid in stages, that structure can allow borrowing to follow actual spending. It does not guarantee that a HELOC will cost less than another form of borrowing: rates, fees, project timing and the amount outstanding all affect the result.

Interest is generally charged on the amount actually borrowed rather than the unused portion of the credit line. But an unused HELOC is not necessarily cost-free. CFPB notes that plans can include annual or membership fees, inactivity fees and other charges.

How borrowing during the draw period works

The draw period is the period during which the line is available for borrowing under the plan's terms. CFPB gives ten years as an example of a possible draw period; it is not a universal HELOC term. Your lender's disclosures should state the actual draw and repayment periods.

Payment rules also vary. Some plans may permit minimum payments that do not repay much principal during the draw period, while others work differently. Regulation Z requires HELOC disclosures to explain how minimum periodic payments are determined and to warn when minimum payments may not fully repay principal or may result in a balloon payment.

Illustration: staged borrowing

Suppose a project is expected to cost $40,000, with $10,000 due initially and later payments due as work is completed. Drawing only the amounts needed at each stage can leave a smaller balance outstanding during the early part of the project than drawing the entire $40,000 on day one. Whether that produces a meaningful saving depends on the actual rate, timing, fees and repayment pattern.

When the draw period ends, further borrowing stops and the account enters repayment according to the agreement. CFPB warns that monthly payments are often significantly higher during repayment, and some plans can require the outstanding balance to be repaid when the repayment period begins.

What determines the borrowing cost

HELOCs usually have variable interest rates. A variable-rate plan commonly uses an index plus a lender-set margin, subject to the terms in the agreement. Prime is a common index, but it is not used by every HELOC and the Federal Reserve's published Bank Prime Loan rate is not an average HELOC offer.

Instead of embedding temporary national-average rates in this renovation guide, we keep current benchmark context on our HELOC Rates & Prime Rate page. For an actual project, compare current offers and the disclosures that explain the index, margin, introductory pricing, rate limits and payment formula.

Fees matter too. CFPB says HELOCs can include application fees; origination, appraisal, title or other closing costs; annual or membership fees; inactivity fees; early-cancellation fees; and fixed-rate conversion fees. The charges depend on the plan.

Plan the renovation before drawing

A useful financing comparison starts with the project rather than the available credit line. Before drawing funds, consider documenting the expected scope, contractor payment schedule, materials costs and a contingency for changes or overruns.

For larger projects, written contractor estimates can help you understand when money will actually be needed. That makes it easier to compare staged HELOC borrowing with a lump-sum loan and to avoid treating an approved credit limit as permission to expand the project.

Keep records if tax treatment may matter

If you intend to claim a mortgage-interest deduction, records showing how HELOC proceeds were used can be important. Current IRS guidance ties potential deductibility to qualifying use of the borrowed funds and other requirements.

Risks to plan for

1
Your home secures the debt

A HELOC uses the home as collateral. CFPB warns that if you cannot repay according to the agreement, you could lose the home. Renovation value or expected resale gains do not remove that repayment risk.

2
The rate can change

HELOCs usually have variable rates, so borrowing costs and payments can change according to the contract. Do not assume every HELOC moves immediately after a Federal Reserve decision or that every plan uses Prime.

3
Renovations can run over budget

Changes in scope, materials and contractor costs can increase the amount you need. A larger HELOC limit does not make a larger project affordable; additional draws create additional secured debt.

4
The repayment phase can change the payment

The end of the draw period can materially change required payments. Review the lender's repayment formula before borrowing, not only the minimum payment required during the renovation.

5
Available credit is not guaranteed forever

CFPB notes that a lender may restrict additional HELOC borrowing in certain circumstances, including a significant decline in the home's value or qualifying changes in financial circumstances. A long project should not assume that every unused dollar of the line will necessarily remain available.

Tax considerations for home improvements

HELOC interest is not automatically deductible merely because the money was spent somewhere on the property. Current IRS guidance says interest on a home equity loan or HELOC secured by a main or second home may be deductible when the borrowed funds are used to buy, build or substantially improve the home securing the debt, subject to applicable requirements and limits.

IRS Publication 936 explains that an improvement is substantial if it adds to the home's value, prolongs its useful life or adapts it to new uses. Ordinary repairs that simply keep a home in good condition are not necessarily substantial improvements, although repair work performed as part of a qualifying substantial renovation can be treated differently.

The latest Publication 936 available when this article was reviewed is for preparing 2025 returns. Tax law and later tax-year guidance can change, so we do not present its debt limits as a permanent 2026 rule. Check the IRS material for the tax year you are filing and consider professional tax advice for your circumstances.

Our dedicated guide, Is HELOC Interest Tax-Deductible?, explains the issue in more detail.

Equity and underwriting

Using the proceeds for a renovation does not create a special universal qualification standard. Lenders set their own HELOC underwriting and product requirements and may consider property value and existing liens, credit information, income and debts, requested line amount and other permitted factors.

There is no site-wide rule that every applicant must retain exactly 15–20% equity, have a 620 credit score, stay below a particular DTI or provide exactly two years of the same income documents. Those requirements vary by lender and product.

For the concepts behind the calculation, see How Much Equity Do You Need for a HELOC?. For the wider application process, see How to Qualify for a HELOC.
