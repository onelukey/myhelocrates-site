Average HELOC Rates — How to Compare HELOC Offers

Searching for the “average HELOC rate” can be useful for market context, but it can also create false precision. There is no single official national HELOC rate that every lender uses. Published averages can differ because surveys may cover different lenders, borrower profiles, credit-line sizes, locations and dates.

For that reason, MyHelocRates.com keeps current benchmark-rate context on our dedicated HELOC Rates & Prime Rate page rather than copying a changing market average into multiple articles. This guide explains how to interpret rate averages and compare the terms that actually affect a HELOC offer.

What does an “average HELOC rate” mean?

An average or survey rate is a snapshot of a particular set of offers. It can help you understand the general market, but it is not a quote and it does not establish the rate a particular borrower should expect.

Use averages as context, not a promise

Your actual HELOC terms are determined by the lender and the full application. Compare written offers using the same credit-line amount and similar assumptions wherever possible.

Rather than publishing credit-score “rate bands” that imply a score automatically maps to a particular APR, we separate two questions: what is happening to the benchmark used by many HELOCs, and what terms has a lender actually offered you?

Prime Rate and HELOC pricing

Many variable-rate HELOCs use an index plus a lender-set margin. The US Bank Prime Loan Rate is a common index, but it is not the only possible index and your agreement controls how your rate is calculated and adjusted.

Illustrative structure

Index + margin = variable HELOC rate. For example, if a plan used a 6.75% index and a 0.75 percentage-point margin, the resulting rate would be 7.50%. The margin in this example is illustrative, not a claim about a typical lender margin.

The Federal Reserve publishes the Bank Prime Loan Rate in its H.15 Selected Interest Rates release. On the date this article was reviewed, the H.15 release showed a 6.75% Bank Prime Loan Rate. That is a benchmark — not an average HELOC offer.

A Federal Reserve policy decision does not mechanically change every HELOC by the same amount on the same day. Whether and when a HELOC changes depends on the index, adjustment schedule, margin, floors, caps and other terms in the agreement.

Why your offered rate can differ from an average

Lenders can consider a range of information when underwriting and pricing a HELOC. Depending on the lender and product, that can include credit history, income and debts, property value, existing liens, requested credit line and other application information.

Item to checkWhy it matters
IndexThe benchmark used to calculate a variable rate.
MarginThe amount added to the index under the lender's pricing terms.
Introductory rateA temporary rate may differ from the rate that applies later.
Rate limitationsFloors, caps and adjustment rules can affect how the rate changes.
FeesApplication, closing, annual, inactivity, cancellation or conversion fees may apply depending on the plan.
Payment rulesMinimum-payment and repayment terms affect cash flow as well as the interest rate.

There is no universal credit-score-to-rate table. A stronger credit profile may affect eligibility or pricing, but lenders use their own underwriting and pricing criteria. See our HELOC qualification guide for the factors lenders may review.

How to compare HELOC offers

Comparing only the headline interest rate can miss important differences. CFPB guidance tells consumers to review how the annual percentage rate may change and to pay attention to fees and other plan terms.

  • Identify the index and margin. Ask exactly how the variable rate is calculated and when it can adjust.
  • Separate introductory pricing from ongoing pricing. Find out how long any promotional rate lasts and what formula applies afterward.
  • Compare fees. CFPB notes that HELOC plans can include application, origination, appraisal, title, annual, inactivity, cancellation and fixed-rate conversion fees.
  • Check draw and repayment terms. A low advertised rate does not tell you how minimum payments are calculated or what happens when the draw period ends.
  • Compare actual disclosures. Use lender-provided terms rather than assuming a national average applies to your application.
APR needs context on a variable-rate HELOC

APR is useful information, but a variable-rate line can change over time. Read the lender's disclosures to understand the rate formula, adjustment rules and fees rather than relying on one number alone.

Variable rates and fixed-rate options

CFPB says HELOCs usually have variable interest rates, which means payments may change. Some HELOCs allow some or all of a balance to be converted from a variable rate to a fixed rate. CFPB notes that the fixed rate is usually higher than the variable rate but is more predictable.

A fixed-rate feature is plan-specific. Check whether it is available, which balances can be converted, whether a conversion fee applies, and what happens to the remaining variable portion.
