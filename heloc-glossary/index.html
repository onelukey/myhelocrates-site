HELOC Glossary

Key Home Equity Line of Credit terms explained in plain English, with lender-specific figures kept separate from definitions.

A
Annual Percentage RateAPR

The true annual cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage. For a HELOC, the disclosed APR generally reflects the periodic interest rate and does not necessarily include every fee or charge. Compare the APR alongside the plan's separately disclosed fees and costs.

Understand HELOC rates & Prime
Appraisal

A professional assessment of your home's current market value, carried out by a licensed appraiser. Lenders use appraisals to determine how much equity you have and how large a HELOC they'll offer. Some lenders use automated valuation models (AVMs) instead of full appraisals for lower-risk applications.

How equity affects your HELOC limit
Automated Valuation ModelAVM

A computer-generated estimate of a property's value based on recent comparable sales, public records, and market data. Lenders use AVMs as a faster, cheaper alternative to a full appraisal. AVMs are less accurate for unusual properties or in fast-moving markets — if your AVM comes in low, you can request a professional appraisal.

B
Balloon Payment

A large lump-sum payment due at the end of a loan term. Some HELOCs require a balloon payment — where the entire remaining principal balance is due when the repayment period ends. Most standard HELOCs are fully amortising, meaning the balance reaches zero through regular monthly payments. Always check your HELOC agreement for balloon payment clauses.

Basis Pointbps

One hundredth of one percentage point (0.01%). Used to describe small changes in interest rates. A 25 basis point rate increase equals a 0.25% rise. Lenders often express rate discounts in basis points — for example, "50bps off for existing customers" means a 0.50% rate reduction.

C
Closing Costs

Fees paid when a HELOC is established, which may include appraisal fees, title search fees, attorney fees, and origination fees. Many lenders offer HELOCs with no closing costs — but check whether those costs are waived or simply rolled into the rate. Some lenders recoup closing costs if you close the HELOC within a set period (a stated period).

Combined Loan-to-Value RatioCLTV

The total of all loans secured by your home — including your first mortgage and HELOC — divided by your home's current market value, expressed as a percentage. Maximum permitted CLTV is lender- and product-specific; there is no universal HELOC threshold. A lower CLTV generally represents more available equity.

How to calculate your CLTV
Credit Limit

The maximum amount you are authorised to borrow on your HELOC. Set at the time of approval, it is based on your home's equity, credit score, income, and lender policy. You can draw up to this limit at any time during the draw period. Having a high credit limit doesn't mean you should use it all.

Credit Score

A numerical rating (typically 300–850 in the US) that represents your creditworthiness based on your borrowing history. A HELOC lender may consider credit scores and other credit information when deciding eligibility, credit limit or pricing. Minimum score requirements vary by lender and product.

What credit score do you need?
D
Debt Consolidation

Using a single lower-rate loan to pay off multiple higher-rate debts. A common HELOC strategy is to use the line of credit to pay off credit card balances, which may have a different borrowing cost. The risk: credit card debt is unsecured; HELOC debt is secured by your home.

Using a HELOC to pay off debt
Debt-to-Income RatioDTI

Your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income, expressed as a percentage. Lenders use DTI to assess whether you can afford additional borrowing. Lenders may use DTI as one part of underwriting when assessing repayment ability. The debts included and acceptable DTI level can vary by lender and product.

How to qualify for a HELOC
Draw Period

The phase of a HELOC during which you can actively borrow money from your credit line. The length and required payment method depend on the plan; do not assume every HELOC has a 10-year draw period or requires interest-only payments. When the draw period ends, no new draws are allowed and the repayment period begins.

Full guide to the HELOC draw period
E
Equity

The portion of your home's value that you own outright — calculated as your home's current market value minus any outstanding mortgage balance. For example, a $400,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage has $150,000 in equity. Your equity grows as you pay down your mortgage and as your home's value appreciates.

How much equity do you need?
Early Closure Fee

A penalty charged by some lenders if you close your HELOC within a set period of opening it — a stated period. This fee helps lenders recoup the costs of setting up the line. Always check your HELOC agreement for early closure terms before signing, particularly if you're not certain you'll keep the line open long-term.

F
Fixed-Rate Option

Some HELOCs allow you to lock a fixed interest rate on a portion of your outstanding balance, converting it to a predictable fixed-rate sub-account while the rest remains variable. This hybrid approach protects against rate rises on the locked portion while retaining the flexibility of the variable-rate line.

Lenders offering rate-lock options
Freeze (HELOC)

When a lender suspends your ability to make new draws from your HELOC. Lenders can legally freeze a HELOC if your home's value drops significantly, your financial situation deteriorates, or you default on payments. A freeze does not affect your existing balance or repayment terms — only new draws are blocked.

Can a lender freeze your HELOC?
Full Appraisal

A comprehensive property valuation where a licensed appraiser physically inspects your home inside and out and compares it to recent nearby sales. Whether a full appraisal is required, how it is performed and what it costs depend on the lender, property and transaction.

H
Home Equity Line of CreditHELOC

A revolving line of credit secured against the equity in your home. Like a credit card, you can draw funds, repay them, and draw again — up to your approved credit limit, during the draw period. Interest is only charged on the amount you've actually borrowed. HELOCs typically have variable interest rates tied to the Prime Rate.

What is a HELOC?
Home Equity Loan

A lump-sum loan secured against your home equity, with a fixed interest rate and fixed monthly payments over a set term. Unlike a HELOC, you receive all the money upfront and cannot re-borrow. Sometimes called a "second mortgage." Best for single defined projects where you know the exact amount needed.

HELOC vs Home Equity Loan
Hard Inquiry

A credit check that occurs when you formally apply for a HELOC. Unlike a soft inquiry (used for pre-qualification), a hard inquiry is recorded on your credit report and may temporarily lower your score by a few points. How multiple inquiries are treated depends on the credit-scoring model and type of credit, so do not assume a particular HELOC rate-shopping window.

I
Index Rate

The benchmark interest rate to which your HELOC rate is tied. Prime is a common HELOC index, but it is not universal. Your agreement identifies the actual index and explains how changes in that index affect your rate.

Interest-Only Payment

A monthly payment that covers only the interest accrued on your outstanding HELOC balance, with no principal reduction. Some HELOC plans may permit or require minimum payments during the draw period that do not substantially reduce principal, while other payment structures exist. While this keeps payments low, the balance does not shrink — making it critical to plan for the higher payments when the repayment period begins.

Understanding payment shock
Introductory Rate

A temporarily reduced interest rate offered by some lenders for an initial period — often 3 to 12 months — to attract new HELOC borrowers. After the introductory period ends, the rate resets to the standard variable rate (index + margin). Always calculate your payments at the post-introductory rate before committing.

L
Lien

A legal claim a lender holds against your property as security for a loan. When you take out a HELOC, the lender places a lien on your home. If you default, the lender can use this lien to force a sale. Your first mortgage holds a first-position lien; a HELOC typically holds a second-position lien, meaning the first mortgage is repaid first in a foreclosure.

Line of Credit

A flexible borrowing arrangement with a set maximum limit from which you can draw funds as needed, repay them, and draw again. A HELOC is a secured line of credit — secured against your home. Unlike a term loan, you only pay interest on what you've actually borrowed, not the full credit limit.

Loan-to-Value RatioLTV

Your current mortgage balance divided by your home's market value, expressed as a percentage. LTV measures how much of your home's value is mortgaged. A lower LTV means more equity and typically better HELOC terms. For HELOC purposes, lenders look at Combined LTV (CLTV) — which includes both your mortgage and the HELOC.

Calculate your LTV and CLTV
M
Margin

The fixed percentage added to the index rate (e.g. Prime Rate) to calculate your HELOC's total interest rate. For example, if the Prime Rate is 7.50% and your margin is 0.50%, your HELOC rate is 8.00%. The agreement states how the margin is used with the index and any applicable rate limitations; check the plan rather than assuming identical margin rules across HELOCs.

Minimum Draw

The smallest amount you are required to draw when accessing your HELOC. Some plans impose an initial or subsequent minimum draw while others may not. The amount is lender- and product-specific. Check your lender's minimum draw requirements before opening a HELOC, especially if you plan to make small, occasional draws.

O
Origination Fee

An upfront fee charged by some lenders to process and set up a HELOC, which may be calculated in different ways depending on the product. Whether an origination fee applies is lender-specific, so compare it with the plan's other charges.

Outstanding Balance

The total amount you have drawn from your HELOC and not yet repaid. Interest is charged only on the outstanding balance, not the total credit limit. Repaying the outstanding balance during the draw period frees up your available credit to draw again.

P
Payment Shock

A substantial increase in required payments that can occur when a HELOC moves from its draw period into repayment. The size of any increase depends on the balance, interest rate and payment rules in the agreement.

How to avoid payment shock
Prime Rate

The benchmark interest rate used by US banks as a reference for variable-rate lending products, including HELOCs. The Federal Reserve's H.15 release reports the Bank Prime Loan rate. A particular HELOC changes according to the index and adjustment rules stated in its agreement, not simply because a Federal Reserve announcement occurs.

Principal

The original amount borrowed — the amount you actually drew from your HELOC, excluding interest. Whether required payments reduce principal during the draw period depends on the plan. During the repayment period, your monthly payments cover both interest and principal, gradually reducing the balance to zero.

Q
Qualified Residence

For tax purposes, the home that secures a HELOC and against which mortgage interest deductions may apply. Under current US tax law, HELOC interest is only potentially deductible when funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the qualified residence — typically your primary or secondary home.

Is HELOC interest tax deductible?
R
Rate Cap

The maximum interest rate your HELOC can ever reach, regardless of how high the index rate climbs. A lifetime cap protects borrowers against extreme rate rises. Some HELOCs also have periodic caps limiting how much the rate can change in a single adjustment period. Always check what caps apply before signing.

Repayment Period

The phase of a HELOC that follows the draw period, during which no new draws are allowed and the outstanding balance must be fully repaid through monthly principal and interest payments. CFPB guidance notes that some plans schedule repayment over a period such as ten or 20 years, while others can require a larger payment when repayment begins. Actual terms are plan-specific.

Draw period vs repayment period explained
Revolving Credit

A type of credit that can be borrowed, repaid, and borrowed again — up to a set limit. A HELOC is revolving credit, similar to a credit card. As you repay your balance, that credit becomes available to draw again during the draw period. This flexibility distinguishes a HELOC from a home equity loan, which is a one-time lump sum.

Right of Rescission

A federal cancellation right that generally applies when a consumer credit transaction creates or retains a security interest in the consumer's principal dwelling, subject to Regulation Z exceptions. For a covered HELOC, the rescission period is generally three business days; the lender's notice explains the deadline and procedure.

S
Second Mortgage

A loan secured against a property that already has an existing (first) mortgage. Both HELOCs and home equity loans are forms of second mortgage. In the event of default, the first mortgage lender is repaid first from any sale proceeds — making second mortgage lenders higher-risk and sometimes charging slightly higher rates accordingly.

Secured Debt

Debt backed by collateral — in the case of a HELOC, your home. If you default on secured debt, the lender can seize the collateral. This contrasts with unsecured debt (like credit cards), where the lender has no claim on specific assets. Rates and costs differ for many reasons, so collateral alone does not determine the price of credit.

Subordination

The ranking of loan priority in the event of default. Your first mortgage has "first position" — it gets repaid first if your home is sold. A HELOC typically holds "second position." If you refinance your first mortgage while a HELOC is open, the HELOC lender must agree to remain in second position — this is called subordination.

T
Tax Deductibility

Under current US tax law, HELOC interest may be deductible on your federal tax return — but only when the funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan. Using HELOC funds for debt consolidation, personal spending, or other purposes does not qualify. You must also itemise deductions rather than taking the standard deduction.

Full guide to HELOC tax deductibility
Title Search

A review of public records to confirm the legal ownership of a property and identify any existing liens, claims, or encumbrances. Lenders require a title search before approving a HELOC to ensure their lien position is secure. Title search fees are part of HELOC closing costs.

U
Underwriting

The process by which a lender evaluates your application and decides whether to approve your HELOC, and on what terms. Underwriters assess your credit score, income, DTI ratio, employment history, property value, and equity. Timing and documentation vary by lender, application, property review and underwriting process.

Unsecured Debt

Debt not backed by collateral — such as credit cards and personal loans. If you default on unsecured debt, the lender cannot automatically seize your property. Using a HELOC to pay off unsecured debt converts it to secured debt — lowering your interest rate but putting your home at risk for obligations that previously posed no such threat.

Using a HELOC for debt consolidation
V
Variable Interest Rate

An interest rate that changes periodically based on a benchmark index — typically the Prime Rate for HELOCs. The actual index, adjustment timing and rate limitations are specified by the HELOC agreement. Most HELOCs are variable-rate products, though some lenders offer a fixed-rate lock on a portion of the balance.

Today's HELOC rates
W
Withdrawal

The act of drawing funds from your HELOC — also called a draw. You can withdraw funds via bank transfer, cheque, or a HELOC-linked debit card, depending on your lender. During the draw period, advances are subject to the available credit limit and any transaction, minimum-draw or other requirements in the agreement.
